1. How fast has the workforce grown?

Every profession has added people since 2015, but not at the same speed. The streamgraph below shows each profession as a flowing band: the thicker the band, the more practitioners.

Design notes (What / Why / How)

This visualization analyzes multi-year quantitative records of annual practitioner counts categorized by healthcare professions to evaluate long-term sector growth and structural dominance shifts. Using a streamgraph idiom with stacked area marks and monotone interpolation, quantitative years are mapped to the horizontal axis, centered practitioner volumes are mapped vertically, and distinct nominal professions are distinguished through categorical color channels.

2. Who are these 100 workers?

Totals hide the mix. Imagine shrinking the whole workforce down to just 100 people. Most are nurses and midwives; doctors are a smaller group than many people expect.

For every doctor, there are about [ratio] nurses and midwives.

How to read it: each square is one in every 100 practitioners. Squares of the same colour belong to the same profession.

Design notes (What / Why / How)

What: the share of registered practitioners in each profession group (nominal profession, quantitative share). Why: to give a quick part-to-whole sense of workforce composition. How: a waffle (unit) chart where each square mark is 1% and position in a 10×10 grid encodes order, with categorical colour hue distinguishing professions.

This visualization compares demographic proportions across age brackets and gender classifications to evaluate workforce aging and gender distribution. Design notes (What / Why / How)

Proportional symbol map: spatial point locations use geographic latitude/longitude for position and circle area to encode workforce totals.

Choropleth: state polygon geometries are joined with derived quantitative practitioner rates per 100,000 residents (ABS 2021 Census populations), encoded through a sequential blue colour scale to highlight regional coverage disparities.

5. Is any state good at everything?

A state that leads on doctors might trail on nurses. Here, each line follows one state across four professions. Lines that zig-zag show uneven coverage; lines that stay high are rare.

[State] leads on doctors but is near the bottom for [profession].

How to read it: each vertical line is one profession, with the lowest state at the bottom and the highest at the top (the numbers show the actual rates). Click a state in the legend to follow it.

Design notes (What / Why / How)

What: practitioners per 100,000 residents for each state across four professions (quantitative, from workforce data joined with ABS population). Why: to compare states across several professions at once and reveal uneven coverage. How: parallel coordinates, with one vertical axis per profession, each axis scaled from its own lowest to highest state, and one line per state with categorical colour and legend-linked highlighting.

The deepest divide is not between states, but between city and country. This grid compares 15 professions across five remoteness areas, from "Major cities" to "Very remote". It shows where the workload per practitioner climbs, for example for paramedics and doctors in remote and very remote areas.

The further you live from a major city, the more [metric] each [profession] carries.

How to read it: rows are remoteness areas, columns are professions. The darker the red, the higher the value.

Design notes (What / Why / How)

This visualization cross-examines quantitative workforce metrics across nominal healthcare professions and ordered remoteness classifications to identify practice pattern disparities between urban and rural settings. Utilizing a Heatmap idiom with rect marks and embedded text labels, nominal professions are arranged horizontally and ordered remoteness categories vertically, with quantitative values encoded through sequential red color saturation and contrast text.
